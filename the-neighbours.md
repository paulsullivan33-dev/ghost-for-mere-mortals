# The Neighbours

**Outcome: you can place Ghost
among the tools that publish words
to the web, and say in one sentence
what each does that Ghost doesn't.**
Everything in this chapter is
characterisation from the projects'
own documentation — none of these
tools was run for this guide, and
the chapter does not pretend
otherwise.

## WordPress

The giant: a PHP publishing
platform from the same 2013-era
argument Ghost was reacting to,
now a general website engine
powering a large share of the web.
Its plugin and theme economy can
make a site do almost anything —
shops, forums, memberships,
booking. Ghost's trade is the
opposite: one shape of site, a
publication, with members and
newsletters as first-class parts
rather than plugins, and an editor
that stays out of the way. Choose
WordPress when the site must grow
organs Ghost doesn't have; choose
Ghost when it must stay a
publication and you'd rather not
maintain a plugin zoo.

## Static generators — Hugo and kin

[Hugo](https://paulsullivan33-dev.github.io/hugo-for-mere-mortals/)
(this series has a guide) and its
relatives turn a folder of Markdown
into finished HTML, once, at build
time. No database, no server
program, nothing running to attack
or upgrade; hosting is any bucket
of files. They are superb for
documentation and for blogs whose
author is comfortable in a text
editor and a git repository — and
they have no members, no
newsletters, no editor a
non-technical partner would open,
and no draft a reader can't reach
by guessing nothing more than a
build you forgot to exclude. Ghost
is the answer when the publication
needs accounts and an inbox; a
generator is the answer when it
needs to be a folder.

## Hosted Ghost — Ghost(Pro)

The same software, run by the
foundation, priced by tier. You
give up the machine and gain back
the upkeep: upgrades, backups,
mail delivery and the MySQL this
guide's lab avoided are theirs.
Because the software is identical,
everything in this guide except
the installing applies unchanged —
and the exit is a database and a
content folder, which is more than
most hosted platforms offer. It is
the honest benchmark against which
self-hosting must justify itself:
your time, against their tier.

## The newsletter services

Substack and its kin bundle
publication, members, payments and
— their real product — a network:
recommendations and an app that
bring readers to you. Ghost gives
you the machinery and none of the
network; your readers arrive by
your own efforts, and in exchange
nobody takes a percentage of your
subscriptions or a say in your
continued existence. Beehiiv and
ConvertKit sit between: hosted,
newsletter-first, with websites
attached. The question is never
features; it is whose building
your archive lives in — chapter
1's question, wearing a price tag.

## The private-writing tools

BookStack, Joplin, Obsidian and
their kin (all elsewhere in this
series) solve the other problem:
words for yourself or your team,
kept orderly and un-public. They
appear here only to mark the
border. If nobody outside should
read it, Ghost is the wrong tool
no matter how well it runs; if
someday somebody should, it is
the shape of tool you want.
